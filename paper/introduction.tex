\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% =========================================================================

\noindent\emph{Status of this document.}  This is the \textbf{technical
report} of the project: it contains everything the formalization does,
including the variants, the spectral and variance routes, the obstruction
calculus, the function-field analogue (with placeholders marked), the
complete catalogue of dead ends, and the methodology.  The mathematical
results are collected, with proofs, in a short companion paper
(\code{paper/short/} in the repository, compiled PDF attached to each release); nothing there depends on
this report.  Where the two differ in framing, the short paper is the
later and more careful statement.


Euclid's proposition IX.20 of the \emph{Elements} shows that for any
finite set of (distinct) primes, each prime factor of their product plus one is outside the
set~\cite{Hardy2009}: to grow your set of primes, you can pick any factor of their product plus one
(\href{https://open.substack.com/pub/marcellop71/p/a-subtle-misreading-of-euclid?utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web}{a Substack post I wrote on this}).
The \textbf{Euclid--Mullin sequence} (\href{https://oeis.org/A000945}{OEIS A000945}), introduced by Mullin~\cite{Mullin1963}, makes a
definite choice: always take the \emph{smallest} prime factor.
\begin{align}
  a(0) &= 2, &
  a(n+1) &= \text{smallest prime factor of } \bigl(a(0) \cdots a(n) + 1\bigr).
  \label{eq:em-def}
\end{align}
The first twenty terms (0-indexed) are
\begin{gather*}
  \underbrace{2}_{a(0)},\;
  3,\; 7,\; 43,\; 13,\; 53,\;
  \underbrace{5}_{a(6)},\;
  \underbrace{6221671}_{a(7)},\;
  38709183810571, \\
  139,\; 2801,\;
  \underbrace{11}_{a(11)},\;
  17,\; 5471,\; 52662739,\; 23003,\;
  30693651606209, \\
  \underbrace{37}_{a(17)},\;
  1741,\;
  \underbrace{1313797957}_{a(19)},\; \ldots
\end{gather*}
The sequence shows an erratic behavior: small primes appear out of their
natural order ($5$ not until position~$6$, $11$ at position~$11$,
$37$ at position~$17$), while enormous primes---a 7-digit number at
position~$7$, a 14-digit number at position~$8$---appear early.  As of
2025, only 51~terms are known and some primes like $41$ and $47$ are
not yet observed.  Computing further terms requires \emph{complete}
factorization of $\Prod{2}(n)+1$ (where $\Prod{2}(n) \coloneqq a(0)\cdots a(n)$
is the running product): finding any prime factor is not
enough---one must certify that no smaller factor exists.  Since
$\Prod{2}(n)$ grows super-exponentially, this quickly exceeds the reach
of all known factoring algorithms.  By construction, no prime can
appear twice.

\begin{conjecture}[Mullin, 1963]\label{conj:mullin}
Every prime number eventually appears in the Euclid--Mullin sequence.
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture has resisted proof for over sixty years.  The difficulty
is showing that the deterministic $\minFac$ rule eventually \emph{selects} each prime.
Shanks~\cite{Shanks1991} offered a compelling heuristic: projecting the
running product onto $(\ZZ/q\ZZ)^\times$ yields a multiplicative walk,
and if the walk behaves pseudo-randomly it must eventually hit $-1
\bmod q$, making $q$ a candidate for selection.  Our formalization
makes this heuristic precise---CME and CCSB
(Sections~\ref{sec:character}--\ref{sec:ensemble}) are rigorous
formulations of ``the walk behaves pseudo-randomly.''

That the rule matters is not idle speculation: for the variant that takes
the \emph{largest} prime factor, Cox and van der
Poorten~\cite{CoxVdP1968} showed that specific primes ($5, 11, 13, \dots$)
are omitted and conjectured that infinitely many are; Booker~\cite{Booker2012}
proved the infinitude (an elementary proof is in
Pollack--Trevi\~no~\cite{PollackTrevino2014}).

Each step couples the next prime to the full factorization history, creating a recursive dependency that
defeats both probabilistic heuristics and standard sieve methods.

At each step, $\Prod{2}(n)+1$ may have many prime factors, and just the \emph{smallest} is chosen.
So, a target prime~$q$ may possibly divide $\Prod{2}(n)+1$ for many (maybe infinite) $n$,
yet never be selected if a smaller prime always divides $\Prod{2}(n)+1$ as well.
Our formalization tries to make this tension precise.

\paragraph{The accumulator structure.}
The running product $\Prod{2}(n)$ is an \emph{accumulator}: a single
number that commits to the entire sequence history via irreversible
multiplication, analogous to the cumulative digest in a hash chain.
The accumulator poses a challenge~$\Prod{2}(n)+1$; the response
$\minFac(\Prod{2}(n)+1)$ extends the chain; and the updated accumulator
$\Prod{2}(n{+}1) = \Prod{2}(n) \cdot \minFac(\Prod{2}(n){+}1)$ absorbs the
response irreversibly---once a prime enters the product, it divides
every future product and can never appear again
(Theorem~\ref{thm:extinct}).

This accumulator coupling is what makes standard tools fail.  Sieve
methods require approximate independence between the events
``$p \mid m$'' for different~$m$; here successive challenges
$\Prod{2}(n)+1$ share a cumulative history.  Ergodic methods require a
fixed or state-dependent map; here the map at step~$n$ depends on the
full accumulator, not just the current residue.  The walk
reformulation (Section~\ref{sec:walk}) tames this coupling by
projecting the accumulator onto a finite group: $\walkZ{2}{q}{n} =
\Prod{2}(n) \bmod q$ preserves the divisibility information relevant
to~$q$ while discarding the accumulator's combinatorial complexity.
But the information lost in this projection is exactly the source of
difficulty: the walk position determines \emph{whether} $q$ can divide
$\Prod{2}(n)+1$; the full accumulator determines \emph{which} prime is
actually selected.  Every open hypothesis in this paper addresses this gap.

\paragraph{The factoring channel.}
The accumulator structure of the EM sequence is closely analogous to a
cryptographic hash chain: each term is deterministically derived from
its predecessor through an operation---integer factorization---that
destroys algebraic relationships.  In an iterated SHA-256 chain
$x_0, H(x_0), H(H(x_0)), \ldots$, each value is uniquely determined
by the seed, yet consecutive terms pass every reasonable statistical
test for independence.  In the EM sequence, the running product
$\Prod{2}(n)$ determines $\Prod{2}(n)+1$, whose smallest prime factor
becomes the next multiplier; but the $O(\log q)$ bits visible in the
residue $\Prod{2}(n) \bmod q$ cannot control the outcome of factoring the
${\sim}2^n$-bit integer $\Prod{2}(n)+1$.  This \emph{information
bottleneck} is why the multiplier residues behave as if
independent---and why the walk on $(\ZZ/q\ZZ)^\times$ should visit
every element, including the ``death state'' $-1$ that would put~$q$
in the sequence.  The analogy is tighter than it may appear: in both
cases the pseudorandomness claim concerns a fully deterministic process
whose forward map is easy but whose outputs resist structural
prediction---the difference being that $\minFac$ achieves this not by
cryptographic design but accidentally, through the information
bottleneck of projecting a ${\sim}2^n$-bit integer onto $O(\log q)$
bits.  Our formalization makes this intuition precise: we
identify the exact mathematical content---Conditional Multiplier
Equidistribution (CME)---needed to convert ``the factoring channel destroys
correlation'' into a proof of MC, and show it implies the conjecture
through a verified chain of reductions.

\subsection*{The story, told from above}
\label{sec:story}

Before the technical development, here is the whole paper in one
telling; the sections then follow it in order.

\paragraph{One map, one orbit: the Euclid step.}
Everything in this paper is about the dynamics of a single map.  Read
the recursion~\eqref{eq:em-def} not term by term but through the running
product $\Prod{2}(n) = a(0)\cdots a(n)$: the product records the whole
history (the ``bag'' of primes collected so far), and one step of the
recursion does two things---it forms the Euclid number $\Prod{2}(n)+1$,
which is coprime to every prime in the bag, and it multiplies the bag by
that number's least prime factor.  So the running product evolves by a
map that depends on nothing but its current value:
\[
  T(n) \;=\; n \cdot \minFac(n+1),
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  \Prod{2}(n{+}1) = T\bigl(\Prod{2}(n)\bigr),
  \quad \Prod{2}(n) = T^{n}(2).
\]
We call $T$ the \emph{Euclid step}.  Its natural domain is the set $X$ of
squarefree integers: if $n$ is squarefree then $\minFac(n+1)$ is a prime
not dividing~$n$, so $T(n)$ is squarefree again, has exactly one more
prime factor than~$n$, and $T(n)\ge 2n$.  A squarefree $n$ \emph{is} a
finite set of primes, and $T$ is ``add the least prime factor of the
product plus one'': Euclid's construction with Mullin's choice, iterated.
The Euclid--Mullin sequence is the sequence of primes appended along the
orbit of~$2$ (equivalently of~$1$, since $T(1) = 2$), and $a(n{+}1)$ is
the multiplier $T(\Prod{2}(n))/\Prod{2}(n)$.  The \emph{ensemble} of
generalized EM sequences (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}) is the family of
all orbits, $\mathrm{genProd}(m,k) = T^{k}(m)$; the tail identity says
that the shift along the orbit is~$T$; the factor tree
(Section~\ref{sec:variants}) is the multivalued relative
$n \mapsto n\cdot p$ for $p \mid n+1$; the variants are other selection
rules; the function-field analogue is $T$ over~$\FF_p[t]$.  Mullin's
conjecture asks whether the orbit of~$2$ meets every prime, and the
question underneath most of what follows is: \emph{what structure does
$(X,T)$ have, and how much of it does one orbit inherit?}

\paragraph{Two exact projections.}
The method rests on two ways of seeing the orbit through a small window,
both exact.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{The residue walk.}  Modulo a prime~$q$, $T^{n}(2) \bmod q$
  is a multiplicative walk on $(\ZZ/q\ZZ)^\times$ with multiplier
  $\minFac(\Prod{2}(n)+1) \bmod q$, and $q$ appears in the sequence iff the
  walk hits~$-1$ past the stage where the smaller primes are exhausted
  (Section~\ref{sec:walk}; the algebraic precondition, that the
  multipliers generate the group, is \emph{free}).  This makes MC a
  hitting statement, exactly.
\item \textbf{The growth constant.}  $C = \lim_N \log T^{N}(2)/2^{N}$
  exists, satisfies $C(Tm) = 2\,C(m)$ on all of~$X$, and
  $C = 0$ iff infinitely many Euclid numbers are composite~$(\mathrm{C}\infty)$
  iff the sequence is not eventually a tower of primes
  (Section~\ref{sec:cinfty}).  So $C$ semiconjugates $(X,T)$ to doubling
  on $[0,\infty)$.  (There is no invariant probability measure for
  $(X,T)$ at all---trivially, since $T(m) > m$ on a countable set---so
  ``a generic orbit'' has no ergodic-theoretic meaning; the semiconjugacy
  adds nothing to that remark, and MC is invisible from $C$.)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The map.}
Told from above, the paper is a map of what one orbit of~$T$ provably
inherits from the structure of~$T$: a sufficient condition at the top,
a hard floor at the bottom, obstruction theorems on both sides, and a
comparison class isolating what makes the problem hard.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The top: CME.}  Character sums on the walk give the
  machine-verified chain
  $\mathrm{CME} \Rightarrow \mathrm{CCSB} \Rightarrow \text{hits} \Rightarrow \MC$
  (Section~\ref{sec:character}).  \emph{Conditional multiplier
  equidistribution}---along the orbit, for a missing~$q$, the multiplier
  residues are equidistributed on each fiber of the walk position---is
  where the story stops.  It is important to say what kind of statement
  it is: CME, CCSB, DH and their relatives are all quantified over primes
  that never appear, so MC implies each of them vacuously and each is
  \emph{equivalent} to MC (\lean{EM/CME/Equivalences.lean}{cme\_iff\_mc},
  \lean{EM/CME/Equivalences.lean}{walk\_layer\_equivalences}).  They are
  reformulations of MC in the language of the walk, not sufficient
  conditions strictly weaker than it; their value is that they turn a
  hitting problem into an equidistribution problem, which is the shape
  analytic number theory knows how to attack.
\item \textbf{Nothing sits under CME.}  Every attempt to place a weaker
  hypothesis beneath it met one of three fates: it was CME by definition
  (several ``different'' hypotheses collapse by \code{rfl}); it was
  walled by orbit-specificity (two multiplier sequences with identical
  statistics, one of whose walks hits $-1$ and one of whose never does,
  Section~\ref{sec:hard}); or it was \emph{false}.  The population layer
  that earlier drafts placed under CME---equidistribution of $\minFac
  \bmod q$ over a fixed-modulus population---asserts that a
  \emph{convergent} series splits evenly among residue classes; we prove
  the population hypothesis \emph{equivalent} to that identity, which
  Dirichlet's theorem does not touch and which fails by head domination
  (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}, Dead End~\#160).
\item \textbf{The floor: $(\mathrm{C}\infty)$.}  Below MC sits weak MC,
  below that reciprocal divergence, below that ``the least factor is
  sometimes small relative to size'', below that $(\mathrm{C}\infty)$; all
  the implications are proved.  The ladder bottoms out at Sylvester-tower
  primality, a statement of the shape of the Fermat question, and the
  arboreal input a Chebotarev argument would supply is free---the residual
  gap is a size condition on one integer's factorization
  (Section~\ref{sec:cinfty}).  \textbf{MC implies this open,
  Fermat-shaped statement.}
\item \textbf{Obstructions.}  No propagating congruence invariant can
  block a prime under $\minFac$, while the same class of invariants is
  inhabited under $\mathrm{maxFac}$ and machine-checks Cox--van der
  Poorten's omission of~$5$; reciprocity-built invariants and smoothness
  guards fail the same way (Section~\ref{sec:min-max-dichotomy}).  A
  disproof would have to be anatomical, not congruential---and,
  symmetrically, no congruence model can predict the multiplier.
\item \textbf{The comparison class.}  Ensemble, factor tree, two-point,
  $\varepsilon$-random and function-field variants each change one
  thing: almost-all tree hitting is unconditional, the perturbed rule
  captures almost surely yet its $\varepsilon \to 0$ limit is a faithful
  reformulation of MC, Booker's steered variant hits everything,
  $\mathrm{maxFac}$ provably misses (Sections~\ref{sec:variants},
  \ref{sec:function-field}).  The lesson, said once: \emph{selection} is
  where the difficulty lives---access to the factor bag is not the
  problem, the deterministic choice of its least element is.
\item \textbf{Dead ends as the coastline.}  150 documented dead ends
  (Appendix~\ref{app:dead-ends}, the complete numbered catalogue), 29 of them
  witnessed in Lean, trace the
  boundary of what one orbit inherits.  Two recurring themes---population
  statistics do not determine one orbit, and multiplier cancellation does
  not force walk cancellation---are illustrated by finite examples in
  $(\ZZ/5\ZZ)^\times$; most entries are instances of these two, of head
  domination, or of a tool needing structure the walk lacks.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent What the telling changes is the framing.  The project is not
``a reduction of MC to a hypothesis''---that framing died with the
population layer.  It is a complete map of what one orbit of~$T$
provably inherits from the structure of~$T$: exact projections (walk,
growth), an equivalent reformulation at the top (CME~$\iff$~MC), a Fermat-shaped floor
at the bottom ($(\mathrm{C}\infty)$), obstruction theorems on both sides,
and a comparison class isolating selection as the culprit.  Late
additions sharpen the projections: the growth constant is defined for
every seed and \defname{MixedDiversity} (``$(\mathrm{C}\infty)$ from every
seed'') is exactly the emptiness of the $T$-invariant set $\{C>0\}$,
which is null threshold by threshold (\S\ref{sec:seeded-growth},
\S\ref{sec:growth-density}); the two projections are coupled through
one bit only---at composite stages position, residue and size are jointly
free in the ensemble (\S\ref{sec:size-residue}); a second, unscaled
invariant, the relative size $\rho(m)$ of the multiplier, inserts the rung
$\MC \Rightarrow \mathrm{RD} \Rightarrow \rho(2)=0 \Rightarrow
(\mathrm{C}\infty)$ (\S\ref{sec:relative-size}); and on the progression
$m \equiv 1 \pmod{P}$ that actually contains the Euclid numbers, the least
prime outside the bag is selected with relative density exactly
$1/q$---Shanks' heuristic with its correct, biased law, at the population
level (\S\ref{sec:crt-equidist}, ``The bag-conditioned multiplier law'').

\paragraph{The main theorem.}
Our main result is the machine-verified reduction of MC to a single
open hypothesis:

\begin{theorem}[\lean{EM/CME/Reduction.lean}{cme\_implies\_mc}]
\label{thm:main-chain}
$\mathrm{CME} \;\Longrightarrow\;
 \mathrm{CCSB} \;\Longrightarrow\; \MC$,
where CME (Conditional Multiplier Equidistribution) says: along the EM
orbit, for a prime~$q$ that never appears, the multiplier residues
$\seq{2}(n{+}1) \bmod q$ are equidistributed on the fibers of the walk
position.  Because CME is quantified over missing primes, MC implies it
vacuously; hence $\mathrm{CME} \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{CCSB}
\Leftrightarrow \mathrm{DH} \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{SHH} \Leftrightarrow
\MC$ (\lean{EM/CME/Equivalences.lean}{walk\_layer\_equivalences}).
\end{theorem}

\noindent So the theorem is a \emph{reformulation}: MC is equivalent to
conditional equidistribution of the multiplier along the walk of any
missing prime.  Its content is the direction $\mathrm{CME} \Rightarrow
\MC$---a hitting problem becomes an equidistribution problem---and the
mathematical content of this paper is the gap between what is proved
about the orbit and that equidistribution\@.  A hypothesis with content
independent of MC would have to speak about the residues of $\seq{2}(n)$
without conditioning on missingness, uniformly in~$q$; the population
layer that tried to supply one was refuted (Dead End~\#160).  (An earlier draft interposed a
population layer beneath CME---population equidistribution of least
prime factors and a transfer lemma to the orbit; it is retired, since
the population statement is equivalent to a false identity between
convergent series, Dead End~\#160 in Appendix~\ref{app:dead-ends} and
\S\ref{sec:ant-formalization}.)

Alongside the conditional reduction, the formalization contains a
fully \emph{unconditional} density theorem
(\lean{EM/Ensemble/UnconditionalPSCD.lean}{almost\_all\_mixed\_hitting\_unconditional}):
for each prime~$q$, a density-$1$ set of squarefree starting
points~$m$ reaches~$q$ in the factor tree of~$m$; a diagonal argument
(\lean{EM/Ensemble/UnconditionalPSCD.lean}{almost\_all\_mixed\_hitting\_diagonal})
extends this to every prime up to a bound $B(X) \to \infty$
simultaneously (Section~\ref{sec:almost-all-mixed}).  Its analytic input ---
divergence of $\sum_{p \equiv a \ (q)} 1/p$ for every invertible
class~$a$ --- is itself machine-checked from Mathlib's Dirichlet
$L$-function machinery
(\lean{EM/IK/DirichletDensity.lean}{prime\_reciprocal\_class\_divergent}),
so the chain carries no hypothesis at all.

The formalization serves two purposes:
(i)~it guarantees that every reduction is logically sound, and (ii)~it
precisely delineates the boundary between what is proved and what
remains open, preventing the kind of subtle gap that plagues pencil-and-paper
reductions involving multiple interacting hypotheses.  Today's
version of that boundary is drawn after an audit of every hypothesis
in the reduction network against three known failure patterns
(fixed-modulus population equidistribution, uncentered character sums
at the trivial character, placeholder propositions); what survives is
listed in \S\ref{sec:verified-results}.

The intermediate reductions are:

\begin{theorem}[\defname{Single Hit Theorem} --- \lean{EM/Equidist/Bootstrap.lean}{single\_hit\_implies\_mc}]
\label{thm:main}
$\mathrm{SingleHitHypothesis} \;\Longrightarrow\; \MC$.
\end{theorem}

SingleHitHypothesis asks: for every missing prime~$q$, if every prime
below~$q$ already appears in the sequence and the multiplier residues
generate the full group $(\ZZ/q\ZZ)^\times$ (SubgroupEscape,
Property~\ref{def:se}), then $q \mid \Prod{2}(n)+1$ for some~$n$ past
the stage where all smaller primes are excluded.  The proof is by strong
induction on~$p$: the inductive hypothesis ensures all primes below~$p$
have appeared, PrimeResidueEscape (proved elementarily) bootstraps
SubgroupEscape, and a single hit gives $\seq{2}(n{+}1) = p$.

The algebraic precondition---that the multipliers generate
$(\ZZ/q\ZZ)^\times$---is free; the open problem is purely dynamical.
Multiple strategies for producing the required hit are formally verified:

\begin{theorem}[\lean{EM/Equidist/Bootstrap.lean}{dynamical\_hitting\_implies\_mullin}]
\label{thm:dh-main}
DynamicalHitting (DH: the walk hits $-1$ cofinally)
$\;\Longrightarrow\; \MC$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[\lean{EM/Equidist/SelfCorrecting.lean}{complex\_csb\_mc'}]
ComplexCharSumBound (CCSB: character sums of the walk are $o(N)$)
$\;\Longrightarrow\; \MC$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent An independent route bypasses CME entirely:
Visit Equidistribution~(VE)---the walk visits every
residue class with equal frequency---also implies MC through a
separate machine-verified chain
(\code{sve\_implies\_mc}, \code{cancel\_weyl\_implies\_mc}).
VE is stated with a threshold ($q \ge Q_0$) and reaches MC only together
with a finite check below the threshold; like CME it is implied by MC
vacuously.  The two are different \emph{routes}, not independent
hypotheses.

\paragraph{Organization.}
The order follows the map above.
Section~\ref{sec:bag} analyzes the bag-theoretic and
information-theoretic structure of the dynamics of~$T$.
Section~\ref{sec:walk} is the first exact projection: MC as
walk-hitting, the algebraic prerequisites, the first missing prime's
death channel avoidance, and the Single Hit Theorem via the inductive
bootstrap (SubgroupEscape is free).
Section~\ref{sec:cinfty} is the second projection and the floor: the
growth constant~$C$, its vanishing as a complete invariant for
$(\mathrm{C}\infty)$, Sylvester-tower primality, the level ladder of
which the classical $\Phi_3$ death equation is the first rung, the free
arboreal input, and the resulting Fermat-shaped consequence of MC\@.
Section~\ref{sec:character} develops the top: the character-analytic
reduction CME~$\Rightarrow$~CCSB~$\Rightarrow$~MC, the large sieve
infrastructure and the walk telescoping identities.
Section~\ref{sec:ensemble} develops the ensemble of all orbits of~$T$
and shows why nothing sits under CME: the population lens is not weak but
the wrong object (head domination), and the orbit hypothesis stands on
its own.
Section~\ref{sec:spectral-routes} collects the spectral and variance
routes: the Weyl hitting bridge, the SVE~$\leftrightarrow$~VE spectral
identity, the Lyapunov drift analysis, and temporal window
decorrelation.
Section~\ref{sec:receptacle} reads the formalization against the
two-arrow receptacle template.
Section~\ref{sec:min-max-dichotomy} gives the obstructions: no
propagating congruence invariant blocks a prime for the $\minFac$ rule
while the same class is inhabited for $\mathrm{maxFac}$; the
obstruction calculus; and the lift from invariants to proofs, which
stays empty along stage-dependence, size, factor count, smoothness and
reciprocity data.
Section~\ref{sec:variants} is the comparison class: the take-all rule,
the target ladder and smallness family, the random-factor, factor-tree,
mixed and two-point variants, and the ensemble and weak-Mullin variants;
Section~\ref{sec:function-field} the function-field analogue over
$\FF_p[t]$, with an explicit account of what is and is not
machine-checked there.
Section~\ref{sec:hard} explains \emph{why} the remaining hypothesis is
difficult by analyzing dead ends and structural barriers, identifies
the open problems whose resolution would close the conjecture, and
assesses the evidence for and against.
Section~\ref{sec:lean} describes the Lean formalization and
catalogues all verified results (\S\ref{sec:verified-results}).
Appendix~\ref{app:history} collects the historical background;
Appendix~\ref{app:analogies} discusses analogies with
Artin's conjecture, multiplicative walks, Sarnak's program, and
sieve theory;
Appendix~\ref{app:routes} presents additional sieve and spectral routes to MC;
Appendix~\ref{app:dead-ends} catalogues the dead ends;
Appendix~\ref{app:agents} describes the human--AI methodology;
and Appendix~\ref{sec:glossary} provides a glossary of all definitions and hypotheses.
